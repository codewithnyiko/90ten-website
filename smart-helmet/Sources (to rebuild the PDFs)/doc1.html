Smart Helmet: What We Found and What We Need

Prepared by 90Ten Group

Purpose

This document explains, in plain words, what 90Ten Group found in the helmet documents and what we need to know before going further. It is written for Thella Group Services and Imperium Corporate. It is a careful check, not a complaint.

In short

  • The documents we received look edited or translated. We want the supplier's own originals, so we know nothing is missing.
  • We have not seen any South African approvals yet. Without them, a mine may refuse the helmet, or it may not be legal to use here.
  • The helmet sends its information over the phone network or Wi-Fi only. Underground, that works only where the mine has one of them.
  • By default, the helmet sends its information to the supplier's computers. We need to know how to keep it in South Africa.

Are these the factory's own documents?

They look edited or translated, and we cannot tell what changed without the factory's originals.

Example: Imagine a recipe copied from a Chinese cookbook, translated, and put into a new folder with a new cover. The recipe may be right. But if one line was dropped, you only find out when the cake fails. That is why we want to see the original cookbook.

What we saw

  • A word was swapped everywhere. The word "hat" was replaced with "helmet", the way "Replace all" works in a document. It also turned "that" into "thelmet". Interface Document page 3 says "Thelmet is to say" instead of "That is to say". The same happens on pages 4, 17 and 42. The Spec Sheet still says "Hats off to alarm", so it was not edited the same way.
  • A line from a translation program. Spec Sheet, page 9: "Here is the updated table with the 'Command' column translated into English". That is the kind of sentence a chatbot writes before it gives you a translated table.
  • Chinese writing left inside. Interface Document pages 9, 23, 29, 34, 35 and 41, and Spec Sheet page 3.
  • Three names for one helmet. KA-6 on the Brochure, IC-KA6 on the Spec Sheet and EQ-D6 on the User Manual.
  • No date or version number. The Interface Document has neither. Like a contract with no date, we cannot tell if it is the latest.

None of this means anything is wrong. It means we cannot be sure nothing is missing.

Approvals and certificates

We have not been sent any certificates, and a helmet with radios generally needs South African approval before it is sold or used here.

What is ICASA? ICASA is the government body that controls radio signals in South Africa, a bit like a traffic police for the airwaves. The helmet has 4G, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, and all three are radios. Radio devices generally need ICASA's approval, called "type approval", before they can legally be sold or used here. Without it, a mine may refuse the helmet.

Example: A label that says "tested" is not the same as a test certificate. It is like a fire extinguisher: you want to see the certificate that shows who tested it and when.

Where the helmet's information goes

By default the helmet sends its information to the supplier's computers, so keeping it in South Africa needs a step the documents do not explain.

Example: It is like a courier who always delivers to the factory address, unless you change the delivery address on the parcel. The supplier says the address can be changed to our own computer. The document says "there are ways", but never says what they are.

What the documents show

  • When switched on, the helmet calls hm.equiinet.cn to ask where to send its information (Interface Document p3).
  • In the examples, its messages, photos, videos and calls go to supplier addresses (pp4, 11 and 37).
  • The supplier says the system can run fully on our own local computers. We need to know how, and whether anything still goes back to the supplier afterwards.

Why it matters

The information includes who is wearing the helmet, where they are and what the camera sees. In South Africa that is personal information, protected by a law called POPIA. A mine may also refuse to let it leave the country.

Basic security

  • The helmet logs in with only its device number and no password. It is like a building that opens for anyone who knows a door number (Interface Document p10).
  • The examples are not encrypted, and sample passwords are shown (pp4, 5 and 11).

Maps

In an outdoor test, a Google map showed the helmet's position. That works because the helmet can see GPS satellites outside. GPS does not work underground (see the next section). The Interface Document names a Chinese map system (Gaode) on pages 19, 20 and 30, while the Brochure says Google Maps (p2). We need to know which one the helmet really sends.

What the helmet can and cannot do underground

Underground, the helmet can send information only where the mine has 4G or Wi-Fi, and where there is none it keeps what it records on the helmet.

Example: It works like a car dash camera. It records everything onto its card, but you can only watch live if it can connect.

Beacons: the documents do not say which types the helmet supports

A beacon is a small sign fixed to a tunnel wall that says "Level 2, west tunnel". The helmet reads the sign as the worker walks past. If a worker goes missing, the last sign they passed tells rescuers where to start looking.

But there are many kinds of beacon, made by different companies in different formats, like different brands of key fob. The documents say the helmet "can scan the Bluetooth beacons for localization" (Spec Sheet p2; Brochure p2) and has alarms for entering and leaving a beacon (Interface Document p20). They never say:

  • which beacon types or formats it understands (for example iBeacon or Eddystone)
  • how it decides that a Bluetooth device is a beacon and not a phone or headphones (the example report on page 15 lists both)
  • how many beacons it can list in one report, and how far away it can hear them
  • how the zones for the enter and leave alarms are set up (the fence message is only mentioned on page 2, and marked "can be left unprocessed")

The helmet can probably hear any beacon, because its example report lists every Bluetooth device it hears. But the documents do not say how it picks out ours, so we should test with a few beacons before buying many. It is like buying a lock without knowing which keys fit.

Words used in this document

These are the technical words, in plain language.

  • ICASA: the government body that controls radio signals in South Africa.
  • SANS and SABS: SANS are South Africa's own product standards, and SABS is the body that tests products against them.
  • Certificate or approval: a paper from an official body showing that a product was tested, like the certificate that says a church hall is safe for a crowd.
  • POPIA: the South African law that protects people's personal information, such as names, places and video.
  • Beacon: a small device fixed to a wall that sends out a short signal saying where it is, like a sign that says "Main hall" or "Kitchen". The helmet reads it as a worker walks past.
  • GPS: satellites that tell a device where it is on the ground. It works outdoors, but not underground.
  • 4G and Wi-Fi: the two ways a phone gets on the internet: through the mobile network with a SIM card (4G), or through a router (Wi-Fi).
  • Server: a computer that receives information from the helmets and keeps it. Think of the church office, where all the reports are handed in.
  • Control room: the place where someone watches the helmets and answers the alarms.
  • SOS: the emergency button.
